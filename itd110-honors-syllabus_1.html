Welcome, Honors inventor.

In regular ITD 110 you learn to build web pages. In the Honors option you also find out who those pages are for. Over seven weeks you will investigate one question: how do college students want information delivered to them on the web — and does what they prefer actually help them learn?

You will read the research, survey real students, build a prototype learning page from what you find, and teach your results to the class. This syllabus is an add-on to the main Aetherworks Handbook. Every policy in that handbook still applies.

3. The Expedition Question — What You Will Investigate

Big question, focused answer.

The central tension: students often say they want short videos, bullet points, and visuals. Research on multimedia learning suggests that what feels easiest is not always what produces the most learning. Your job is to find where preference and effectiveness agree, and where they don’t.

Pick one angle to focus on

  • Format: video vs. text vs. interactive pages vs. infographics
  • Length and chunking: short micro-lessons vs. full-length pages
  • Navigation and layout: how students find information on a course site or Canvas
  • Device: where and on what device students actually study
  • Accessibility: captions, transcripts, contrast, and readability
  • Preference vs. performance: does the format students prefer help them remember more?

What a strong research question looks like

Too broad: What do students like online?

Focused: Do NOVA students prefer short narrated videos or illustrated text pages for learning a technical concept, and does their preferred format improve short-term recall?

Sample thesis: Students prefer video, but well-designed text with visuals produces equal or better recall at a lower production cost.

Pick one debate to stage

  • Prensky vs. Kirschner & De Bruyckere: are today’s students really different learners?
  • Student preference vs. Mayer and Pashler: should designers give students the format they ask for, or the format the evidence supports?
  • Nielsen and Krug vs. Mayer: do “easy to use” and “easy to learn from” point the same way?

Find sources through the NOVA Library (ERIC and Academic Search Complete), Google Scholar, and nngroup.com. A librarian can help you search in 15 minutes.

9. Thinking Machines — AI on the Honors Project

The course AI policy applies, with a few research-specific rules.

What is allowed

  • AI-assisted code in your prototype, following the course rule: every AI-generated piece must be commented in the code, labeled as AI-assisted, and explained.
  • Using AI to brainstorm survey questions or check your survey for confusing or leading wording. You still write and own the final version.
  • Using AI to help find sources. You must open, read, and verify every source yourself; AI tools sometimes invent citations.

What is never allowed

  • AI-written summaries, responses, synthesis, reflections, or self-assessments. Those must show your own thinking.
  • Invented or AI-generated survey responses or quotes. All data must come from real participants.
  • Breaking these rules carries the same penalty as the course AI policy: a zero, and a report to Academic Affairs.

10. The Inventor’s Code — Research Ethics

You are studying people. Treat them well.

  • Anonymous only. Never collect names, student IDs, or email addresses.
  • Voluntary. Tell every participant what the study is for, that it takes about 3 minutes, and that they can stop at any time.
  • Approval first. Dr. G approves your survey before it launches. [Confirm with NOVA whether class research projects like this need IRB review or an exemption.]
  • Ask before recruiting. Get permission from other instructors before surveying their sections.
  • Report honestly. Report results that contradict your thesis. A “not supported” hypothesis is still a good finding.
