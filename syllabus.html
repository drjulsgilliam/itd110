ITE 152 — Introduction to Digital and Information Literacy

Fall 2026 · Section 008N · In person, on campus
Your Instructor: Dr. Juls Gilliam, D.Sc. — “Dr. G”

Welcome to the workshop.

This course is about becoming fluent with the machine — and just as importantly, fluent with information. You will learn how computers work, how to drive Word, Excel and PowerPoint like a professional, how to tell a credible source from a convincing one, how to keep yourself safe online, and how to build and publish a web page of your own.

Read this handbook all the way through. It takes about fifteen minutes, and it is the only fifteen minutes this semester that can stop a problem before it starts. Get to the bottom and you might find something special waiting for you.

1. Meet Patch — Your Guide for the Semester

You will see him on nearly every page of this course. Here is who he is.

This is Patch. He is the mascot of this course, and he is a builder — goggles pushed up, tools in his bag, a half-finished project on the bench behind him.

Patch exists for one reason: a syllabus is a long document, and the things that matter most in it are easy to skim past. So Patch stands next to them. When you see him holding a sign, that sign is something I do not want you to miss — a deadline, a rule, or a piece of encouragement you may need in week nine.

He is also here because this course asks you to build things, and building is less lonely with company. Patch is not good at everything either. That is rather the point.

What Patch is

A guide. He points at the thing on the page that matters most, he keeps the tone of this handbook friendly, and he shows up in Canvas so you always know you are in the right course.

What Patch is not

He is not an AI you can ask questions. He cannot grade your work, extend a deadline, or answer an email. Anything you need a decision on comes to me — Dr. G.

Where you will see him

On this handbook, on the course homepage, in module headers, and on the check-in questions. When Patch is holding a sign, read the sign — it is usually the part students miss.

What runs Patch: curiosity, courage and kindness.

Curiosity is asking why something works, not just whether it worked. Courage is posting the question in front of the whole class, or trying the harder version of the assignment. Kindness is how we treat each other when somebody’s code breaks in front of everyone — and it will, and it will be mine some weeks.

Those three are the actual standard for how this room runs. Everything in the policies section is downstream of them.

One honest note about Patch

Patch is an illustration. He was made with AI image tools, and I am telling you that plainly because this course spends a whole module on using AI honestly — it would be strange to model anything else. The words in this handbook are mine, the decisions are mine, and the person responsible for your grade is a human being with an office in CC 120F.

Course Description

Develops understanding of digital and information literacy. Introduces basic computer concepts in hardware, software, cyber, cloud, database, and operating systems. Includes hands-on experience developing word processing, spreadsheet and presentation documents. Evaluates the reliability of sources. Covers creating a simple web page. Examines topics such as social, legal, and ethical issues.

Prerequisite

Students must be able to read and write at a college level.

Technical Competencies

You need basic Canvas knowledge and the ability to navigate a computer using Windows or macOS. You should be comfortable using the Internet and Canvas. Review the Canvas tutorial in your course shell if you are new to it.

3. The Blueprint — What You’ll Learn

If you do well in this course, you will be able to do all of this.

Basic Computer Concepts

  • Explain the importance of the history of computers.
  • Define and identify computer hardware and software.
  • Recognize the specifications for purchasing PC hardware and software.
  • Describe the benefits of protecting systems while online or working remotely.
  • Describe computing systems and how they are used to support and promote organizational goals.
  • Recognize how computers are used in different careers.
  • Define key terms: router, modem, app, input device, output device, storage device.

Operating Systems

  • Recognize the different versions and capabilities of multiple operating systems.
  • Demonstrate the use of an operating system: create folders, create nested folders, save files and organize files.

Digital and Information Literacy

  • Define, understand, and explain the need and impact of information literacy in today’s society.
  • Self-assess and identify information needs.
  • Identify different types of information sources.
  • Develop and use search strategies to effectively perform searches to acquire sources from various platforms.
  • Apply techniques to fact-check and evaluate information and sources, such as the CRAAP test.
  • Describe intellectual property, copyright, and fair use, and how they relate to using information legally and ethically.
  • Recognize various forms of plagiarism and use skills and techniques to avoid plagiarism.
  • Distinguish between credible and non-credible digital sources.

Critical Thinking

  • Determine the nature and extent of the information needed.
  • Identify and evaluate information for credibility, currency, reliability, validity, accuracy, usefulness, relevance, and bias.
  • Distinguish between assumption and fact.
  • Incorporate information literacy skills into your own knowledge base and work.

Current Software Applications

  • Use APA style (7th edition) to format research papers, document in-text citations, and create an annotated bibliography.
  • Create documents using word processing formatting and editing tools.
  • Develop worksheets and charts incorporating formulas, functions, and formatting.
  • Apply spreadsheet design principles to worksheets and tables, and use data analysis.
  • Use conditional and logical functions and cell referencing in spreadsheet software.
  • Create and modify presentations, enhancing them with pictures, shapes, media and animation.
  • Identify and describe database key principles: primary key, tables, fields, records, rows, and creating, modifying and maintaining a database.

Web Technologies

  • Identify introductory web design and development concepts.
  • Identify top-level domain use and purpose (.gov, .edu, .com, .net, .org).
  • Describe what responsive design means, and explain HTML, CSS, JavaScript and tags as they relate to developing websites.
  • Define hosting and publishing in web development.
  • Develop a simple webpage using a text editor or HTML editor containing basic tags such as title, headers, links, multimedia and images.
  • Explain the importance of web development in world technology, and the value of basic knowledge of website creation.

Cyber Awareness and Information Security

  • Define cyber security and explain why it matters personally, on the Internet, for computers and for mobile devices.
  • Describe the challenges of securing information.
  • Identify types of attackers that are common today.
  • Describe the different types of attacks.
  • Define different types of defenses.

Cloud Computing and Emerging Technologies

  • Define cloud computing, explain the cloud computing stacks, and identify its advantages and disadvantages.
  • Define the Internet of Things (IoT).
  • Explain the current version of the global cellular standard.
  • Define blockchain and the components of a block, and discuss how blockchain could be used across industries.
  • Compare progressive web apps and traditional web apps, and compare the Internet to Internet2.
  • Discuss the different aspects of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and identify other emerging technologies.
  • Give examples of how data science incorporates technology, and identify careers in computing.

Communication, Collaboration & Civic Engagement

  • Communicate information legally and ethically using a variety of channels directed at a range of audiences.
  • Collaborate with other students to manage information using information technology.
  • Demonstrate competency in creating and delivering a presentation, and in linking and embedding data in a presentation.

Quantitative & Scientific Literacy, Professional Readiness

  • Use logical formulas to better understand the importance of becoming a logical thinker in the world of computers.
  • Use scientific or mathematical formulas to understand how a computer can assist with computations.
  • Recognize the importance of an Internet presence.
  • Demonstrate productivity using application software.
  • Explain the importance of cyber and information security.
  • Identify the role the computer plays across professional areas of expertise.

How points work — the whole rule

Nearly everything in this course is worth 100 points. Every exam, every project, every discussion, every activity. Same number, every time, so you never have to do arithmetic to work out whether something matters.

There is one exception. The Emerging Technologies Group Presentation is worth 200 points — double everything else. I am telling you in week one because every semester a few people decide the group project is the one they can afford to skip. It is the most expensive thing to miss in the entire course.

What Goes in Each Category

Projects · 30%

The six builds listed below. These are the pieces that end up in your portfolio.

Exams · 30%

Three of them, each taken at home with a full week to complete it. You demonstrate skills, not recall.

Assignments · 25%

Discussions, simulations and the in-class activities. Many small chances to bank points.

Participation · 15%

Attendance, what you do once you are here, and answering me when I write to you.

Participation is 15%

It is not a personality score, and you do not have to be the loudest person in the room. It is a record of whether you did these seven things:

  • Be here. In the room, on time, on Monday and Wednesday.
  • Stay for the whole class. Leaving at the halfway point counts the same as not coming. The second half is usually where we build the thing.
  • Take part in your own learning. Have the file open, follow along, ask when you are lost, answer when I ask.
  • Do the group work. Show up for your team, carry your share, and do not make three other people cover for you.
  • Learn the applications. Word, Excel, PowerPoint and the web tools — hands on the keyboard, doing it yourself, not watching someone else do it.
  • Finish the in-class assignments. They are started and finished during class, and they cannot be made up if you were not there.
  • Answer me back. If I leave feedback on your work that asks you something, or send you an email that needs a reply, respond within 24 to 48 hours. I am not asking for an essay — one sentence is enough. Almost every problem in this course is fixable while it is still small, and I cannot fix a problem I am talking to myself about.

That is the whole thing. Do those seven and the 15% takes care of itself. Miss a stack of classes and this is the category that quietly sinks an otherwise good grade.

Communication Is a Professional Skill

One purpose of college is to prepare you not only with technical knowledge, but with the professional skills you will need after you graduate. One of those skills is communicating when something goes wrong.

In school, at work, in relationships, and in professional settings, problems will happen. Avoiding communication almost always makes them harder to solve.

You do not have to agree with every piece of feedback. You do not have to have the perfect response. You do not have to know how to solve the problem before you contact me.

You do need to communicate.

My goal is to help you practise asking questions, receiving feedback, raising concerns, advocating for yourself, and communicating professionally even when the situation is uncomfortable.

How to Write to Me

Email is a professional document, not a text message. In this course you will practise writing one properly, because the way you write to me is the way you will one day write to a manager, a client, or a hiring committee.

Every email you send me should have all six of these:

  1. A subject line. Start it with ITE 152 – 003N, then a few words about what you need.
  2. A salutation. Open with *Hi Dr. G,* or *Dear Dr. Gilliam,* — Dr. G is what I answer to. Not “hey,” not “yo,” and not nothing at all.
  3. Complete sentences. Capital letter at the start, punctuation at the end, one idea per sentence. Watch for run-ons — if you need to take a breath in the middle, it is probably two sentences.
  4. Nothing that looks like texting. No *u, ur, idk, plz, cuz, tysm*. No sentence that runs six lines with no full stop. No paragraph made entirely of lower-case letters.
  5. Readable formatting. 12 point or larger, in a sans-serif font — Arial, Calibri or Verdana. Black text on a white background. No decorative fonts, no coloured backgrounds, no images behind the words.
  6. Your name at the end. Sign it. Your email address is not a signature, and I should not have to work out who you are from the header.

Run spellcheck before you send. Send from your NOVA email account — it is the only address I am able to reply to. Ask one clear question with enough detail that I can answer it without needing a second email from you.

Standards for Everything You Submit

Unless an assignment tells you otherwise, every document you hand in should meet these standards. They are not fussiness for its own sake — they are the baseline that professional work is expected to meet everywhere you go after this course.

  • 12 point or larger, in a sans-serif font — Arial, Calibri or Verdana.
  • Complete sentences. A complete sentence has a subject and a verb and ends with punctuation.
  • No run-on sentences. Two complete thoughts need either a full stop between them or a proper joining word. Long is not the same as thorough.
  • Spellcheck is required. Every application we use in this course has it built in. Turning in work you never ran through it tells me you did not reread it.
  • Proofread with your own eyes afterwards. Spellcheck cannot tell *form* from *from*, or *their* from *there*.
  • Your name, the course, and the assignment title go on the document.
  • Submit the file type the assignment asks for, through Canvas, before the deadline.
  • Keep it professional. Write as though a future employer will read it — because a version of this is exactly what they will read.

One More Thing: I Am a Human Being

There is a person behind this grade book. I read what you write. I notice who is here and who has gone quiet. I remember what you told me in week three about the shift you picked up at work. I am not a system that returns a number.

I care how you do in this course, and I care how you are doing generally. That is the whole reason the standards above exist — not to catch you out, but because I would rather teach you to communicate well now, while the cost of getting it wrong is a comment on an assignment instead of a job you did not get.

Being professional is not the same as being cold. It is how we show each other respect. You can be warm, funny, confused, frustrated or overwhelmed in a message to me — just be all of those things in complete sentences, with my name at the top and yours at the bottom.

Be professional with me and I will be professional with you. Be human with me and I will be that too.

9. Boiler Room Rules — Course Policies

Not the fun part. Still the part that protects your grade.

Before you read this section, one thing worth saying plainly: none of it is here to catch you out. I have taught this course enough times to know what the term looks like for students who do well and for students who struggle, and almost every rule below is me trying to move you toward the first group.

Show up, start early, tell me when something is wrong, and you will almost never meet the sharp end of any of this. If you do run into trouble, come and talk to me. I can nearly always help with a problem I know about, and I would much rather hear about it early than read about it in a final grade.

Education is a cooperative effort between you and me. I plan a variety of learning activities to help you master the course content. Your contribution is to take part in those activities within the framework set out in this syllabus.

Attendance

  • You are expected at every class meeting.
  • More than 3 unexcused absences lowers your final grade by one letter grade.
  • Being late counts. Arriving late counts as half an absence. If it becomes a pattern I may ask you to wait for a natural break before joining us, so the class is not interrupted — but do talk to me if something makes arriving on time genuinely hard, and we will work it out.
  • If you will be late or absent, email me ahead of time or as soon as you can.
  • You are responsible for all missed material when you are not in class.

If you miss the first class, come and find me.

It happens, and it is completely fixable. Email me and we will set up a short Zoom, or drop into office hours — whichever is easier for you.

Day one is when we go through this syllabus together: how the course runs, what I expect, and the rules we all work by. Missing that day does not put you outside those rules. They apply to you from the moment you are on my roster. What it does mean is that you would be guessing at them — and in my experience that is the single most common reason a student runs into trouble later in the semester.

So rather than let that happen, I ask you to book the meeting. We go through the rules properly, you get to ask me anything you like, and you start the semester knowing exactly where you stand instead of finding out the hard way.

Please treat this one as required rather than optional: I am not able to keep you in the course if we have not had that conversation. I would far rather spend twenty minutes with you now than lose you over a missed first day.

Good news: if you attend consistently and show real effort, I may add small participation or effort points when your final grade is right on the line between two letters.

In-Class Assignments

Several assignments in this course are in-class assignments — the hardware activity, the OS and file management assignment, the Word and Excel activities, and the cybersecurity and cloud activities. They are started and finished during class, they are graded, and they count.

If you are not in class, you cannot make up an in-class assignment.

There is one exception: you contact me before that class meeting. Not after. If you know ahead of time that you will miss a class, email or call me in advance and we will work something out.

Reach out ahead of time and I will do what I can. Reach out the day after, and most likely the answer is that the assignment cannot be made up — not because I am being difficult, but because the work only exists in the room, with me there to help. There is genuinely nothing left to hand in afterwards. So tell me in advance, even if it is an hour in advance, and we will sort it out.

Turning Work In on Canvas

  • All assignments go through Canvas (or MindTap where noted) by the posted due date and time.
  • If you submit late, Canvas automatically takes off 20% per day.
  • Emailed assignments are not accepted unless I specifically ask for one.
  • If I asked you to revise something, or gave you permission to turn it in late, email the finished assignment straight to me at jgilliam@nvcc.edu.
  • Check Canvas every day for updates and announcements, especially before class.

If you see a zero, do not panic.

Until I have graded something, Canvas shows it in the gradebook as a zero. That is not your grade. It only means I have not marked it yet, and it will change as soon as I do. Please do not read it as a judgement on your work — it is just how Canvas displays an ungraded item.

I have seven days to grade an assignment, and in a busy week I use all of them. If you turned your work in late, it joins the queue behind everything that arrived on time, so please do not expect an instant turnaround on it.

Why 11:59 PM is a trap

NOVA has more than 75,000 students. Uploads fail, Canvas slows to a crawl near deadlines, clock differences mark work late, large files take time, it is easy to submit the wrong file when rushing, and there is no time left to check that your submission opens.

Do this instead: submit 30 to 60 minutes early, then open your upload to make sure it works.

10. Thinking Machines — The AI Policy

Read this one twice. The penalty here is the harshest in the course.

The rules, in four lines

  1. AI tools are permitted only as a supplemental resource for learning the content — or when I explicitly assign them.
  2. Submitting completed work generated by an AI tool will be treated as plagiarism.
  3. A submission generated by an AI tool receives a grade of 0 with no redo.
  4. Submissions are run through AI-detection software. It is extremely accurate.

Never use AI to write your explanations, reflections, discussion posts, or your research paper. Those exist to show what you understand.

When AI is part of the assignment

Module 9 is about artificial intelligence and multimedia. In that module you will deliberately use AI tools to create images, a song and a video, and we will discuss the ethics of doing so. When AI is the assignment, I will say so plainly in Canvas.

Outside of those moments, treat AI the way you would treat a tutor: it can explain a concept to you, and it cannot do the work for you.

Why the line is drawn here

AI matters more and more in IT work, and employers expect people entering tech careers to understand how it works and when it is appropriate. But this course exists to build your judgement about information — whether a source is credible, whether a claim is a fact or an assumption, whether a document says what you meant. An AI tool cannot build that for you. Using it to skip the work means leaving the course without the one thing it was designed to give you.

Academic Integrity PolicyCheating, plagiarism, AI, and what happens next

NOVA promotes and emphasizes the importance of honesty in academic work. It is therefore imperative for students to maintain the highest standard of honor in their scholastic work.

Academic dishonesty, as outlined in more detail in the Academic Integrity Policy (Policy Number: 224), can include, but is not limited to cheating on an exam or quiz, submitting work that is not your own (plagiarism), or sharing assessments online. Students are urged to be cautious when using any web tools designed to assist with assignments without their instructors’ explicit permission to do so and to cite their sources in all their work.

Consequences of academic dishonesty can include a failing grade on an assignment, a failing grade in the course, and may include additional administrative sanctions such as suspension or expulsion from the college. Procedures for disciplinary measures and appeals are outlined in the Academic Integrity Procedures. It is a student’s responsibility to become familiar with the student code of conduct. Lack of awareness is no excuse for noncompliance with NOVA’s policies and procedures.

NOVA faculty may choose to use tools such as Turnitin to detect and flag instances of plagiarism in academic writing.

NOVA announces campus and college closings on the NOVA homepage. You can also receive notification by cell phone or email if you register for NOVA Alert. Also review NOVA’s guidance on emergency closings, delayed openings, and continuation of instruction.

If a course is canceled due to a weather event or other unforeseen situation, check the course Canvas site or NOVA email as soon as possible for instructions and assignments to avoid falling behind in coursework.
